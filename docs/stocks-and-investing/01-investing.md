
## Investment basics

### terminology

- **market cap**: the total value of a company in the market, found by multiplying its stock price by the number of shares
- **index**: A group of preselected investments used to measure the performance of a market sector, like DOW, NASDAQ and SP500
- **blue chip stock**: a well-established company stock that has reliable performance over decades
- **penny stock**: a stock you can buy at a low price and is considered high risk

#### What to invest in based on market cap

Based on market cap, you have these categories:

- **micro cap**: companies valued at less than $300M in the stock market
	- Investing in micro cap companies is high risk high reward, since they're considered as penny stocks.
- **small cap**: companies valued from $300M - $2B in the stock market
- **large cap**: companies valued over $10B
	- Investing in large cap companies is low risk low reward, because you will not see much growth from larger companies.

#### Tax-sheltered accounts

Both Pre-Tax (Traditional) and Post-Tax (Roth) retirement accounts enjoy **tax-sheltered growth** while invested—meaning you pay $0 in capital gains, dividend, or interest taxes year over year in both account types. T

he fundamental difference between them is not how fast they grow, but **when you pay income tax**: upfront today (Roth) versus upon withdrawal in retirement (Pre-Tax).

- **when to use pre-tax**: When your current tax bracket is significantly higher than your expected tax bracket in retirement, **Pre-Tax contributions yield higher net gains and overall wealth**.



#### Taxable brokerage account

You invest after-tax money and then you pay **capital gains tax** on the interest you earned in the stock market.

- **Capital Gains Tax:** This is a tax on the profit made when you sell an asset (like a stock) for more than you paid for it, basically a tax on the gains you made on a stock, including dividends.

Here is how to reduce capital gains tax:

- **hold the stocks for longer**: holding stocks for **longer than a year** can qualify you for lower, long-term capital gains tax rates

#### Tax efficiency

Tax efficiency refers to the specific tax advantages or benefits available for each type of investment that can help you pay less tax.

- **dividend paying stocks**: invest in these stocks only within a ROTH IRA tax-free account, because otherwise you'll have to pay capital gains tax on the dividends you earn if investing into a brokerage account. 
- **max out tax-sheltered accounts**: each year, max out 401K, Roth IRA, and HSA 
- **Tax-loss harvesting:** In taxable brokerage accounts, you can sell investments that are currently at a loss to offset any realized capital gains. You can then immediately repurchase a similar asset to maintain your original market exposure.


### ETFs vs mutual funds

- **Mutual Fund:** This is described as a pool of investors who share both the risk and the reward by collectively buying a diverse range of companies and assets
- **Exchange Traded Fund (ETF):** Similar to a mutual fund, an ETF holds a basket of underlying assets—such as stocks, bonds, or cryptocurrencies—but it is designed to be traded on a stock exchange, making it highly liquid and easily managed

There are differences between the two:

- **Costs and Access:** ETFs generally have lower costs and no minimum investment requirements, whereas many mutual funds may have minimum investment amounts
- **Variety and Management:** Mutual funds often offer a wider variety of specialized or actively managed options, which is sometimes required for certain retirement accounts like 401ks

### Which ETFs to invest in

The best ETFs to invest in have these criteria:

#### ETF fees

ETFs have a percentage fee per year which they take from all your investment money in that fund, ranging from 0.03% - 1% of your holdings.

Here's a common misconception:

>"Choose high fee ETFs with a 1% fee, since they'll perform better and the fee won't be as damaging."

But in reality, a higher fee does **not** equal higher performance. 

- **evidence shows low-fee ETFs outperform high-fee ETFs**: Decades of SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) data show that over a 10-year period, **85% to 90%+ of active funds charging 0.50%–1.00%+ fees underperform low-cost index funds** (like VOO/VTI at 0.03%).
- **High fees actively erode compounding wealth from day one.** On a $100,000 balance, a 1% fee costs $1,000 _every single year_ just in management fees, regardless of whether the fund goes up or down. Over a 10-year FIRE horizon, that fee drag compounded destroys tens of thousands of dollars in gains.

#### ETFs that pay dividends

You want to invest in stocks that pay dividends further down the line.

- **Total Return vs. Dividend Yield:** During your accumulation phase (Age 22–32), your goal is maximizing overall portfolio growth (**Total Return = Capital Appreciation + Dividends**). Dividend-focused funds (like SCHD/VYM) deliberately filter for mature, cash-distributing companies (banks, utilities, consumer staples) and exclude high-growth companies that reinvest earnings into expansion.
    
- **Dividend Irrelevance:** When a company pays a dividend of $1/share, its share price drops by $1 on the ex-dividend date. Dividends are not bonus returns; they are forced liquidations of company equity.
    
- **Tax Efficiency in Taxable Accounts:** If held in a taxable account, dividends trigger annual taxes (qualified dividend tax rates up to 15%–20%), creating tax drag. Dividend funds are better suited during the _decumulation_ (spending) phase if income generation is needed, not during the rapid wealth-building phase.

## Budgeting

### Creating a budget 

A budget should be separated into three categories:

- **needs**: rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, etc.
- **wants**: entertainment, gym, dining out, short term goals, etc.
- **savings**: emergency fund, investment

The best proportion for a monthly budget is the 50-30-20 rule, where you divvy up your monthly income to go to these weighted categories:

- 50% needs
- 30% wants
- 20% savings

### Rules of budgeting


1. Track every expense you have
2. Save up enough money in your bank account for an emergency fund worth 3-6 months of income.
3. For short-term goals of stuff you want to buy, always create SMART goals and then set up automated savings for those goals.
4. Have a savings first mindset. Set up automated investing and savings
5. Follow the [[#1% rule]] to avoid making large impulsive purchases.

### Financial Goals



To create short-term budgeting goals for things that you want to save for or buy, you should create SMART goals following this format:

- **specific**: a specific thing you want to buy or save for
	- **example**: a new macbook pro
- **measurable**: has a numerical monetary value attached to it
	- **example**: a new macbook pro that costs 3.6K
- **achievable**: achievable within the timeframe you set for yourself
	- **example**: completely achievable to save for a $3.6K macbook in three months if you save $1000/month
- **realistic**: you can realistically and practically achieve it now, it doesn't rely on ideals or something you don't have yet.
	- **example**: completely realistic to save for a $3.6K macbook in three months if you save $1000/month and you earn $5000/month after tax.
- **time-bound**: put a timeframe on it.

Here are the rules for short-term goals:

- **convert expensive wants to short-term goals**: You should create SMART short term goals from expensive wants in your budget or financial goals you want to reach. use the 1% rule to classify whether a "want" should be transferred into a short-term goal rather than an impulsive purchase.
- **short-term goals are achievable in less than 3 years**: A short-term financial goal is something you can achieve in less than 3 years.
- **short-term goals should have liquid savings**: to save for a short-term goal, you must save into liquid assets like checking accounts.

Then to achieve those short term goals, do the following:

1. calculate the amount you need to save per month to achieve that goal by the due date
2. Automate those savings into a liquid account (not stocks)

## FIRE

FIRE is a movement to retire early by saving as much money as possible and then investing all of it in low-cost ETFs and then living off 4% of your yearly returns of your stock portfolio.

### Rules

The 4% rule tells you when it's okay to retire, when you can live off 4% of your yearly returns of your stock portfolio. It's basically that number where you can then start to retire because now you can live off your returns, accounting for inflation.

- **example**: if you spend $40K per year in expenses, that is 4% of 1M dollars, so you can retire when you have $1M invested.


> [!NOTE]
> This comes from actual research: retirees can always withdraw 4% from their portfolio yearly even through the worst stretches the market has ever seen. 


![](https://i.imgur.com/re3PTnj.jpeg)



In summary, here are the rules all FIRE methods follow:

1. Invest in low-cost ETFs
2. Max out Roth IRA
3. Max out traditional 401K


### investment for FIRE

To retire at age 32 while retaining liquidity before age 59½, save 80K-90K per year and then build a **three-bucket portfolio**:

1. **pre-tax 403B/401K**: invest $24.5K/yr pre-tax, save extra 7.1K and invest that.
2. **roth IRA**: invest $7.5K/yr after-tax
3. **taxable brokerage accounts**: Save $48K/yr, since capital gains are 0% up to $48.3K/yr

### Methods

Here are the different methods:

- **traditional FIRE**: invest 30-50% of your after-tax income yearly, retire at the 4% rule.
	- **timeline**: Assuming $100K income after tax, this method will take you 13-15 years to retire.
- **COAST FIRE**: spend 7 years in the traditional FIRE path, and then stop contributing to your retirement account, let it compound. Take up a lower paying job that you like.
	- **timeline**: Assuming $100K income after tax, this method will take you 7 years to retire.
- **frugal FIRE**: invest 90% of your after-tax income yearly, retire at the 4% rule.
	- **rules**: to achieve this extreme savings, never eat out, don't have any streaming services, no unnecessary expenses, find a cheap place to live.
	- **ratio**: live off 20-30% of your after-tax income, invest the rest
	- **timeline**: Assuming $100K income after tax, this method will take you 9 years to retire.
- **divided FIRE**: go traditional FIRE but by investing only in stocks that pay dividends so you get passive income for the rest of your life so you don't need to withdraw from your retirement account.
	- **timeline**: You would need $1.25M invested into dividend-paying stocks in order to get 50K/year in dividends, meaning with traditional FIRE you could retire in 20 years.
- **slow FIRE**: invest 15-20% of your income after taxes, retire at the 4% rule
	- **timeline**: Assuming $100K income after tax, this method will take you 27 years to retire.
